iDesign method and Clean Architecture are compatible at the boundaries but collide in the middle. The friction here is sharper than it is with hexagonal architecture, and for a specific reason: hexagonal is silent about how you structure the core, so iDesign slots straight into the empty space. Clean is *not* silent — it prescribes Entities and Use Cases as concentric rings, and that prescription lands on exactly the ground volatility-based decomposition wants to occupy.

**Where they align.** Both insist that business logic never touch a database, a UI, or a third-party service directly, and both enforce it the same way: an inner component declares a contract and an outer component implements it. Clean's gateway interfaces — defined in the use-case ring, implemented out in Interface Adapters — are functionally identical to iDesign's ResourceAccess contracts, right down to the motivation (swap the store without touching the rules). Both are also explicitly use-case aware, which makes the comparison closer than the hexagonal one: Clean has a dedicated Use Cases ring of Interactors, and iDesign has Managers whose whole job is to encapsulate workflow. Both forbid dependencies pointing outward — Clean by the Dependency Rule, iDesign by its closed layering and call rules — and both keep cross-cutting concerns (logging, security) orthogonal to the main decomposition rather than smeared through it.

**Where they differ.** The distinction is what *justifies* a component's existence. Clean decomposes by **level of policy**: how close a piece of code sits to the business versus to the machine. That gives you rings, and within the rings you tend to get one Interactor per use case and one Entity per business noun. iDesign decomposes by **volatility**: a component exists to encapsulate something that changes independently, and its name should describe an area of change rather than a business object. Löwy is direct about the consequence — decomposing by domain entities is, in his reading, functional decomposition wearing a domain costume, and it produces systems that shatter when requirements shift. Clean's innermost ring institutionalizes precisely that decomposition.

The granularity disagreement follows from the same root. Clean practitioners routinely write one Interactor per use case, and nothing in the model discourages it; large Clean codebases with several hundred Interactors are common. iDesign treats that as the primary failure mode: Managers encapsulate a *family* of use cases, the guideline is a small handful of them, and a Manager count that tracks the use-case count is the signal that The Method was not actually applied. Löwy would also point out that Clean stops at code structure, whereas The Method continues into project design — staffing, effort, the critical path, and the planning artifacts that make the architecture buildable. That is simply outside Clean's remit.

**Combining them in practice.** The reconciliation is better than it first looks, because Clean's ring ordering is itself a volatility argument. Martin justifies Entities as the innermost ring on the grounds that enterprise rules are *least likely to change when something external changes* — which is a stability claim, not an ontological one. Read that way, Clean orders **layers** by volatility while iDesign decomposes **components** by volatility. Same principle, different axis, and they can be applied together:

- Entities ring = the genuinely stable enterprise invariants only — the rules that would survive the application being rewritten. Not a home for every business noun.
- Use Cases ring = Managers (workflow per use-case family) plus Engines (business-rule and activity volatility), interacting only through contracts.
- Interface Adapters = Clients on the inbound edge; ResourceAccess implementations on the outbound edge.
- Frameworks & Drivers = Resources — the actual databases, queues and third-party APIs — plus the delivery mechanism.
- Utilities sit orthogonally, as they do in both models.

Structured that way, each service satisfies the Dependency Rule internally, while the question Clean never answers — *which components should exist at all* — gets answered by volatility analysis rather than by enumerating use cases and entities.

**One caution.** The failure mode specific to this pairing is having two homes for business rules. If you keep a populated Entities ring *and* a set of Engines, rules will drift between them and nobody will be able to say which is authoritative. Decide once: either Entities hold the invariants and Engines hold only volatile activity logic, or you collapse Entities to data contracts and let Engines own the behaviour. The second is more faithful to The Method; the first is easier to sell to a team already fluent in Clean. Either is defensible — carrying both taxonomies at full strength is not.
