iDesign method and Hexagonal Architecture type are compatible in spirit and can even be combined, but they operate at different levels and have some philosophical friction. Let me break it down.

**Where they align.** Both are reactions against naive functional/technical layering, and both share core goals: isolating business logic from volatile infrastructure, enforcing dependency discipline, and making change cheap. Hexagonal architecture (ports & adapters) says the domain core must not depend on databases, UIs, or external services — those are adapters plugged into ports. iDesign says the same thing structurally: Managers and Engines (your business logic) never touch a database or external system directly; all such access goes through ResourceAccess components, and clients talk to Managers through contracts. An iDesign ResourceAccess component is, functionally, a driven-side adapter, and its interface is essentially a port. Both also insist that dependencies point away from volatile details — hexagonal via dependency inversion toward the core, iDesign via its closed layered call rules and contract-based interaction.

**Where they differ.** The key distinction is what drives the decomposition. Hexagonal is a *dependency topology*: it tells you where the boundary between core and outside world sits, but says little about how to structure the core itself. iDesign is a *decomposition method*: volatility-based analysis tells you what the components are, and the Manager/Engine/ResourceAccess taxonomy tells you their roles and allowed interactions. So they're answering different questions — hexagonal answers "which direction do dependencies point," iDesign answers "what components exist and who may call whom."

There's also a philosophical difference in flavor. Hexagonal grew out of the DDD-adjacent world, where the domain model is the center of gravity and use cases orbit it. iDesign is use-case-first: Managers encapsulate workflow volatility per use case family, Engines encapsulate activity/business-rule volatility, and Löwy is notably skeptical of domain-driven decomposition — he'd classify decomposing by domain entities as a form of functional decomposition, which The Method explicitly rejects. So a purist DDD-hexagonal practitioner and a purist iDesign architect would argue about *what belongs in the middle of the hexagon*, even while agreeing entirely about keeping SQL out of it.

**Combining them in practice.** A pragmatic mapping that many teams effectively use, whether they name it or not:

- The hexagon's inside = Managers + Engines (workflow and business rules), interacting only via contracts.
- Driving ports/adapters = the client-facing edge: REST controllers, message listeners, UI backends calling Manager contracts.
- Driven ports = ResourceAccess contracts; driven adapters = the ResourceAccess implementations wrapping SQL, blob storage, third-party APIs.
- Utilities (logging, security) sit orthogonally, as they do in both models.

Structured this way, each iDesign service internally satisfies the hexagonal rule (core depends only on abstractions; infrastructure is swappable at the edges), while the system-level decomposition — which services exist at all — comes from volatility analysis rather than from domain modeling. That's a coherent and fairly common hybrid: iDesign for the macro architecture and project design, ports-and-adapters as the micro-architecture discipline inside each component or service.

**One caution.** Don't try to enforce both taxonomies as separate physical layers simultaneously (e.g., a "ports layer" plus a "ResourceAccess layer" doing the same job) — you'll get ceremony without benefit. Pick iDesign's vocabulary for component roles and recognize that hexagonal's constraint is already satisfied by iDesign's call rules and contract discipline. The compatibility is real precisely because hexagonal is a constraint iDesign structures already meet, not an alternative structure competing for the same slot.
